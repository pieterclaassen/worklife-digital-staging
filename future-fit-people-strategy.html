People strategy

Is your people strategy future-fit?

Why sustainable people management starts with measurement, not assumptions.

Psychology · People Strategy · Sustainability · Coaching
5 June 2025 · Dr Anna-Rosa le Roux

Organisations rarely become disengaged all at once.

The shift is usually gradual. A missed commitment here. Less energy in meetings. Cameras increasingly off. Managers avoiding difficult conversations. Teams talking more about “them” than “us”.

None of these signals, on their own, tells you what is wrong. But together they may point to something important: the conditions supporting performance are beginning to weaken.

That is why a future-fit people strategy cannot begin with assumptions about what employees need. It has to begin with evidence.

Notice the early signals

Organisational strain can show up in different ways:

  • reduced discretionary effort or collaboration;
  • poor follow-through on commitments;
  • increasing withdrawal from team interaction;
  • higher absence or patterns of lateness;
  • managers postponing feedback or avoiding conflict;
  • unclear communication about strategy and priorities; and
  • a shift from a sense of shared ownership to “we versus they”.

The temptation is to respond quickly with another engagement initiative, leadership programme or wellbeing intervention. But before spending more, leaders should ask a more basic question:

Do we understand what is actually driving the problem?

Is your people investment aimed at the right problem?

A useful people strategy should answer three questions.

1. Is it strategically aligned?

People investment should support what the organisation is trying to achieve. Growth, transformation, innovation and operational resilience place different demands on leadership, capability and culture.

If the organisation is recycling legacy initiatives without understanding its current pressure points, even well-designed programmes can miss the real issue.

2. Can you see whether it is working?

Activity is not the same as impact. Participation rates, programme attendance and survey completion tell you that something happened. They do not necessarily tell you whether leadership improved, strain reduced, capability grew or performance became more sustainable.

Useful measurement combines quantitative evidence with qualitative insight and gives leaders something they can act on.

3. Does it create value for the people it is meant to support?

An intervention can make perfect sense on paper and still fail in practice. Coaching may be offered when the real issue is role clarity. A wellbeing programme may be launched when workload or leadership behaviour is the actual source of strain.

Employee voice matters because it helps distinguish between what leaders intend and what people experience.

Measure the drivers, not only the outcomes

Engagement, retention and performance are outcomes. Leaders also need to understand the conditions producing them.

Those conditions may include psychological safety, meaningful work, quality of leadership, belonging, autonomy, clarity, trust and the ability to recover from sustained demands.

This is where generic pulse surveys can fall short. They may tell you what people are reporting without helping you understand why.

Better diagnostics allow organisations to ask more useful questions:

  • What is helping people perform well?
  • Where is strain accumulating?
  • Which leadership or organisational practices are contributing?
  • Why are critical people staying - or leaving?
  • Where would intervention create the greatest leverage?

Measurement is not the end point. Its value lies in improving judgement.

Wellbeing is part of organisational performance

Wellbeing is sometimes treated as a benefit offered to employees. A more useful view is to see it as one of the conditions that shapes whether people can sustain attention, judgement, collaboration and effort over time.

That shifts the question from “What wellbeing programme should we buy?” to “What in the way work is designed, led and experienced is helping or hindering sustainable performance?”

This is also consistent with the World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work, which emphasises organisational interventions and manager capability alongside individual support.

From insight to intervention

Once the organisation understands what is happening, the intervention becomes much easier to choose.

Sometimes the answer is role redesign. Sometimes it is leadership accountability, better feedback, clearer priorities or changes to workload and decision-making. Sometimes professional coaching is useful because it helps leaders and employees examine the beliefs, habits and behavioural patterns influencing how they work.

Coaching is most valuable when it is connected to a real organisational need. It can help people translate insight into different behaviour, strengthen judgement and bridge the gap between knowing and doing.

But the principle remains the same: diagnose first, intervene second.

Building a future-fit people strategy

A sustainable people system is not a collection of programmes. It is the set of organisational conditions that enables people to contribute, adapt and perform over time.

That requires leaders to:

  • measure what matters;
  • understand the psychology of work;
  • connect people investment to strategic priorities;
  • design with people rather than simply for them; and
  • evaluate whether interventions are creating the intended value.

The future-fit question is therefore not whether your organisation has enough people initiatives. It is whether you have enough evidence to know which ones matter.
