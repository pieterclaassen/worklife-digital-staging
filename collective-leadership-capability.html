The Hidden Risk in Your Business Strategy: Collective Leadership Capability

Understanding the behavioural architecture that enables or constrains strategy execution.

25 November 2026 · Dr Anna-Rosa le Roux

Some of the most expensive problems in organisations are the ones leaders struggle to see.

An organisation may have a compelling business strategy, sufficient financial resources, sophisticated technology and a highly experienced executive team. Yet its ability to execute that strategy may be constrained by something that has received considerably less attention: the collective behavioural capability of its leadership population.

When executive teams develop business strategies, they typically assess financial, technological and operational readiness. They evaluate market opportunities, investment requirements and commercial risks.

But how systematically do they assess whether their existing leadership population is behaviourally equipped to deliver that strategy?

As organisations prepare for 2027 and beyond, this question becomes increasingly important.

Artificial intelligence, technological disruption, changing workforce expectations and increasing societal demands require organisations to become more adaptive, collaborative and innovative, while sustaining the human and organisational capacity on which future performance depends.

What if the leadership characteristics that made your organisation successful are the very characteristics that could constrain its future?

When past success becomes a strategic risk

Consider an organisation investing significantly in digital transformation.

Its future operating model requires experimentation, rapid learning, cross-functional collaboration, distributed decision-making and a willingness to challenge established business practices.

However, its leadership population has been selected, developed and rewarded over many years for operational discipline, risk management, technical expertise and hierarchical accountability.

These characteristics have contributed significantly to the organisation's success. They remain valuable organisational assets.

Yet the future strategy requires a different balance of leadership behaviours.

Without understanding this potential misalignment, the organisation may invest heavily in technology, operating model redesign and change management, while overlooking the behavioural conditions that could undermine all three.

There is a scientific explanation for how this happens.

Organisational psychologist Benjamin Schneider's Attraction-Selection-Attrition framework explains how organisations can become relatively homogeneous over time through the people they attract, select and retain.

Leadership promotion and succession practices may reinforce this process. Organisations promote individuals who succeed within their existing operating models and frequently use successful incumbent leaders as reference points when identifying future talent.

The unintended consequence is that organisations may reproduce the leadership characteristics of their past rather than intentionally develop the behavioural diversity required for their future.

What appears to be a strong and coherent leadership culture may gradually become a source of strategic rigidity.

Could your leadership population be constraining your strategy?

Collective leadership capability gaps rarely present themselves as clearly identifiable leadership problems.

They are more likely to become visible through recurring business challenges.

These observations are not necessarily evidence of personality-related problems. They may have structural, operational, technological or cultural explanations.

However, when such challenges persist despite repeated interventions, executives may benefit from examining the underlying behavioural patterns within their leadership system.

The commercial implications can be significant: delayed strategic implementation, underutilised technology investments, duplicated effort, slow decision-making, ineffective leadership development expenditure and the gradual erosion of organisational capacity.

The risk is not simply ineffective leadership. It is the cost of a strategy that the organisation struggles to execute.

Having good leaders is not the same as having collective leadership capability

Organisations invest considerable resources in identifying, assessing and developing individual leaders.

Yet an organisation can have highly capable individual leaders and still struggle collectively with collaboration, innovation, adaptability and decision-making.

A leadership population characterised by strong personal ambition and competitiveness may deliver exceptional functional results while struggling to achieve enterprise-wide outcomes.

Leaders with a strong preference for structure and predictability may excel in established operational environments but collectively struggle to respond to disruptive change.

A highly confident leadership population may provide decisive direction while being insufficiently receptive to contradictory information, emerging risks or alternative perspectives.

None of these characteristics is inherently problematic.

Their strategic relevance depends on the demands of the business, how they are expressed and whether complementary capabilities are available within the leadership system.

Collective leadership capability is therefore not simply the sum of individual leadership capabilities.

It emerges through the interaction of individual characteristics, relationships, team dynamics and organisational conditions.

This distinction has important implications for how executives approach leadership development, succession planning and strategic transformation.

The behavioural architecture of strategy execution

I propose that organisations consider what I refer to as their behavioural architecture: the configuration of individual characteristics, collective behavioural patterns and organisational practices through which leadership is exercised and strategy is translated into action.

Understanding this architecture requires examining three interconnected levels.

Individual - What behavioural resources do our leaders bring?

Understanding the personality characteristics, behavioural strengths and potential vulnerabilities that influence how leaders respond to complexity, uncertainty, interpersonal demands and pressure.

Collective - How do our leaders operate together?

Understanding how leadership characteristics complement or reinforce one another, how authority is exercised, how decisions are made and whether sufficient behavioural diversity exists to respond to emerging strategic demands.

Organisational - What does our system enable or constrain?

Understanding how organisational culture, governance, performance expectations, reward mechanisms and structures influence the expression of leadership capability.

These levels cannot be adequately understood in isolation.

An organisation may encourage collaboration while rewarding individual achievement. It may advocate innovation while penalising unsuccessful experimentation. It may promote empowerment while concentrating decision-making authority.

Under these conditions, even leaders who possess the behavioural capability to act differently may struggle to do so.

The implication for executives is important: not every leadership problem requires a leadership development programme.

Some require a different organisational system.

Making collective leadership capability visible

Scientifically grounded personality assessments, such as the Hogan Personality Inventory (HPI) and Hogan Development Survey (HDS), provide a useful starting point for examining aspects of an organisation's behavioural architecture.

The HPI provides insight into everyday personality tendencies that influence how leaders approach work, relationships and professional challenges.

The HDS identifies potential behavioural risks that may become counterproductive under conditions of pressure, fatigue or reduced self-monitoring.

While these instruments assess individuals, appropriately aggregated and interpreted findings can generate hypotheses about behavioural patterns across the leadership population.

When integrated with leadership effectiveness measures, employee feedback, team diagnostics, organisational culture assessments and business performance evidence, these insights can help executives explore questions such as:

  • Which behavioural resources are well represented within our leadership population?
  • Where might strategically important capabilities or complementary perspectives be underrepresented?
  • What potential behavioural risks could become more pronounced during transformation?
  • Where do leadership patterns differ across functions, business units and organisational levels?
  • Are our organisational practices enabling or constraining the leadership behaviours our strategy requires?

The objective is not to establish an ideal personality profile or to categorise leaders as suitable or unsuitable for the future.

It is to develop an evidence-informed understanding of the behavioural resources, potential vulnerabilities and developmental priorities within the leadership system.

Personality data alone cannot establish collective leadership effectiveness or predict strategy execution. Its value lies in how it is integrated with other organisational evidence and interpreted within the strategic context.

Rather than introducing a generic leadership development programme, it could determine which constraints require individual coaching, which require collective leadership interventions and which require changes to organisational structures, governance or performance management.

The organisation could preserve the capabilities that have contributed to its success while deliberately strengthening those required for its future.

This is the difference between investing in leadership development and investing in strategic execution capability.

Five executive decisions informed by collective leadership diagnostics

A Collective Leadership Capability Review could inform five important areas of executive decision-making.

1. Strategy execution and risk management

Identify potential behavioural constraints before committing significant resources to strategic transformation. Incorporate collective leadership capability into implementation planning and strategic risk discussions.

2. Leadership development investment

Direct development expenditure towards the behavioural capabilities most critical to the business strategy. Differentiate between individual, collective and systemic development requirements.

3. Succession planning and talent acquisition

Determine whether the leadership pipeline is reproducing historical strengths or developing the capabilities required for the future. Identify opportunities to introduce or develop complementary behavioural resources.

4. Organisational transformation

Understand whether strategic implementation is being constrained by individual leadership capability, collective behavioural patterns or the organisational systems within which leaders operate.

5. Sustainable organisational performance

Examine whether existing leadership practices are building or depleting the human and organisational capacity required for future performance.

Together, these insights enable organisations to approach leadership capability as a strategic asset that can be understood, developed and more effectively deployed.

How would executives know whether the investment is making a difference?

Leadership development is frequently evaluated through participation rates, programme completion and participant satisfaction.

While these measures provide useful information, they tell executives relatively little about whether the organisation is becoming more capable of delivering its strategy.

A strategic approach to leadership capability development should establish relevant baseline measures and track changes over time.

Depending on the strategic priorities, these might include:

  • Speed and quality of decision-making.
  • Cross-functional delivery against strategic milestones.
  • Adoption of new technologies and operating practices.
  • Employee perceptions of leadership effectiveness, trust and psychological safety.
  • Succession readiness against future leadership requirements.
  • Employee wellbeing, engagement and retention.
  • Relevant operational and commercial performance indicators.

The purpose is to establish whether developmental investments are contributing to observable behavioural change and improved organisational outcomes.

Although attributing commercial outcomes to leadership interventions requires careful evaluation, executives should expect a clearer connection between leadership investment and the strategic objectives it is intended to support.

Putting leadership capability on the strategic agenda

Before committing significant resources to strategic transformation, executive teams should consider undertaking a Collective Leadership Capability Review.

Such a review would examine the behavioural resources available within the existing leadership population, identify potential risks to strategy execution and explore the organisational conditions that enable or constrain leadership effectiveness.

It would provide a foundation for more informed decisions about leadership development, succession, talent acquisition, organisational transformation and sustainable performance.

The intention is not to add another assessment process to an already crowded people agenda.

It is to integrate leadership capability into the same strategic conversations in which organisations evaluate financial resources, technology, operational capacity and business risk.

An organisation's leadership population represents a significant investment of experience, knowledge, relationships and human potential.

Understanding and developing that collective capability may be one of the most important investments an organisation can make in its future.

The question is not simply whether your organisation has good leaders.

It is whether your organisation has the collective leadership capability to deliver the future it is investing in.
