The Hidden Risk in Your Business Strategy: Collective Leadership Capability

Your strategy may be sound. The more difficult question is whether your leadership system is behaviourally equipped to execute it.

25 November 2026 · Dr Anna-Rosa le Roux

Most organisations invest heavily in strategy. Far fewer invest with the same discipline in the leadership capability required to execute it.

Plans are built around markets, customers, technology, capital and operating models. Leadership teams are then expected to deliver - often on the assumption that strong individual leaders will automatically produce strong collective execution.

That assumption is risky.

A leadership team can contain highly capable individuals and still struggle to make decisions, challenge constructively, work across boundaries, adapt under pressure or align around difficult trade-offs.

This is where strategy often encounters a less visible constraint: collective leadership capability.

The behavioural architecture of strategy execution

Every strategy places behavioural demands on the organisation.

A growth strategy may require faster decisions, greater collaboration and more distributed authority. A transformation strategy may require leaders to let go of practices that made them successful in the past. A cost or performance agenda may demand sharper prioritisation, accountability and the ability to challenge established assumptions.

I think of this as the behavioural architecture of strategy execution: the patterns of thinking, deciding, relating and leading that must exist for the strategy to become real.

These patterns are rarely visible in a strategy document. But they determine whether leaders can translate intent into coordinated action.

Individual talent is not the same as collective effectiveness

Many organisations assess leadership one person at a time. That is useful, but incomplete.

Strategy is rarely executed by individuals acting independently. It is executed through relationships, decisions, dependencies and competing priorities across a leadership system.

A team may therefore have strong individual capability while still showing collective weaknesses such as:

  • avoiding difficult conversations;
  • slow or unclear decision-making;
  • protecting functional interests at the expense of enterprise outcomes;
  • over-reliance on a small number of influential leaders;
  • limited challenge or excessive consensus;
  • poor coordination across boundaries; or
  • leadership behaviours that fit the current business but not the future one.

These are not simply interpersonal issues. They can become execution risks.

When past success becomes a future constraint

One of the more difficult leadership questions arises during transformation.

The leaders who delivered today's success may have developed their capability in a very different organisational context. Their strengths may have been shaped by a particular operating model, market, culture or set of performance expectations.

A new strategy can change the behavioural requirements of leadership significantly.

The question is therefore not whether current leaders are good or bad. It is whether the leadership capability that exists today is aligned with what the future strategy will require.

Successful incumbents can become future constraints when the behavioural demands of the strategy change faster than the leadership system does.

Diagnose before you develop

When execution becomes difficult, organisations often move quickly to leadership development, coaching, team building or restructuring.

Those interventions may be useful. But without diagnosis, they can also become expensive activity aimed at the wrong problem.

A Collective Leadership Capability Review starts elsewhere. It asks:

  • What does the strategy require leaders to do differently?
  • Which behaviours and decision patterns will be critical to execution?
  • Where does the current leadership system already have the required capability?
  • Where are the gaps, tensions or dependencies?
  • Which roles or individuals may be well suited to the future - and which may require support, repositioning or succession planning?
  • What organisational conditions are reinforcing the current patterns?

The purpose is not to produce another leadership scorecard. It is to create evidence that improves strategic judgement.

What this allows leaders to decide

Once the leadership requirements of the strategy are clearer, organisations can make more targeted decisions about:

  • leadership team composition;
  • succession and critical-role risk;
  • where development investment will create the greatest value;
  • which leaders need coaching or transition support;
  • where decision rights or governance may need to change;
  • how transformation should be sequenced; and
  • what leadership behaviours need to be measured over time.

This turns leadership capability from a broad development agenda into a question of strategic readiness.

The hidden risk

Boards and executives routinely review financial, operational, market and technology risks. Leadership capability is often discussed, but less often examined as a collective execution risk with the same level of rigour.

That matters because the quality of the strategy does not remove the need for people to interpret it, challenge it, make trade-offs, coordinate across boundaries and act under uncertainty.

The strategic question is not simply whether you have strong leaders. It is whether your leadership system has the collective capability to deliver the future you are asking it to create.
